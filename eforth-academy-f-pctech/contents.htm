PREFACE TO THE SECOND EDITION:

 

OUT OF THE SHADOW OF F83

 

 

 

 

 

It has been almost a year since F-PC 2.25 was released.  It is very encouraging to see that F-PC spread rapidly inside the Forth community.  It is also surprising that F-PC reached many new users who have not considered using Forth because of its perceived weaknesses and limitations.  Due to the fact that we were able to immobilize Tom Zimmer from further modifying and enhancing this version, many veteran Forth programmers were convinced that they had a solid ground to build useful applications.  We have seen many substantial programs converted and developed in this environment.

 

However,  Tom could not be held steady for long.  There were reported bugs in F-PC he had to fix, and there were great ideals to be put in to enhance its performance.  In the end, a compromise was stricken that he could make changes anyway he thought necessary, but the modified system could not be called F-PC and F-PC would stay at Version 2.25 until November, 1989.  He distributed the modified versions under the name F-TZ, whose version number clicked from 2.25 to 3.34.  The changes and the enhancements were very impressive, especially the hypertext help system and the source code browser.  Bob Smith also cleaned up the help files associated with the KERNEL files.  When the F-PC Working Group reconvened in its August, 1989 meeting, it decided unanimously to adopt F-TZ 3.34 as F-PC 3.5.

 

When F-PC was first released, it was necessary to emphasize its heritage of F83 in order to capitalized on the popularity of F83, and to acknowledge the fact that much of the source code in the kernel were simply lifted from F83.  In the course of enhancing F-PC, Tom went through every word and managed to change most of them for one reason or another.  He left no stone unturned, without a single thought about how difficult it is to update the documentations.  One of the major reasons to change the kernel so extensively was the new target compiler Tom built.  It has to compile words independently into the target code image.  In F83, there were lots of shared code, which is executed by several words to reduce the size of the dictionary.  The new target compiler cannot detect that a word must jump to other words, and consequently every word must be completely self-contained.  Therefore, all kernel words using shared code were flattened.  Another reason of modification is performance.  A good example is the addressing of multiple items on the return stack.  Tom developed three styles: indexing through BP register, indexing through DI register, and swapping the SP and BP registers and then using PUSH and POP instructions.  The fastest method was chosen in each instance when the return stack is manipulated.  The outer shell of F83 (the names of words) is preserved, but everything underneath was changed.

 

In F-PC 2.25, much of the F83 tools to access block file like LIST, LOAD, VIEW, etc. are retained in spite of the switch from block files to sequential files.  These tools are less meaningful in the new F-PC 3.5, because of the much more powerful browsing facility and the hypertext help system.  In F83, as in most other Forth systems, the text interpreter is the programming environment and the editor is an application to provide services on the manipulation of the source code.  In F-PC 3.5, much of the programming environment is shifted into the editor.  The editor allows you to compose source text, and to explore the system.  You will spend as much time in the editor as in the interpreter.

 

At this point, F-PC is ready to assert its own identity, equal to if not surpassing F83.  I hope that it will become a common vehicle by which many Forth programmers will use to build applications and also to communicate ideals and algorithms.

 

My last comment on F-PC is about its size.  F-PC 3.5 has more than 2600 words, making it the largest public domain Forth system.  It is also larger than most commercial Forth systems.  To put it in perspective, figForth has about 350 words, and F83 has about 1000 words.  The largest commercial Forth system I know is Harvard Softworks' HS/FORTH, which has about 1700 words.  The growing size is demanded by the increases in functionality and also in convenience to the user.  However, the convenience comes not without a price, which includes greatly increased demands on memory and disk storage, and the demand on the user in the comprehension of the entire system.  F-PC is very friendly to the user in doing routine Forth programming.  However, like other large language compilers, it is difficult to modify to suit specific needs.  It is amazing that the whole system would work at all.  The only reason that it works at all is the tireless efforts Tom Zimmer put into it.

 

Friendliness is all in the eyes of the beholder.  F-PC proves that Forth can be as friendly as any other systems if you care to put in the features that the user needs.  Incorporating all the features most programmers need result in a mammoth system, which adds little to a programming language and Forth in particular.  The essence of Forth is modularity, interactivity, and simplicity.  These qualities, though preserved in F-PC, are obscured by the huge volume of F-PC.  Lots of tools, utilities, and conveniences (like all the earthly possessions) give you only a false sense of security.  It is the complete understanding of the bare essentials which gets the job done.

 

A couple of years ago, Chuck Moore released his cmForth for the Novix 4000 Forth chip.  Many people were amazed at what he accomplished in 30 screens: a complete Forth system with an optimized compiler and a target compiler.  After studying cmForth carefully, my feeling could only be expressed using the words from Lao-Tze, the legendary progenitor of Toaism:

 

    "For knowledge, add a little everyday;

     for wisdom, erase a little everyday."

 

Here we have two contrasting Forth systems.  By adding a little everyday, Tom Zimmer built F-PC with tons of features and utilities.  Polishing off all the non-essential elements in Forth, Chuck Moore gave us the functional definition of Forth in its purest form.

 

So, go ahead and enjoy F-PC.  While you are doing it, keep in mind that the Tao of Forth is not very far and it can always be reached with some time and efforts.

 

C. H. Ting

 

November 1989

 

 

 

PREFACE TO THE FIRST EDITION:

 

FOOLISH OLD MAN MOVING A MOUNTAIN

 

 

 

 

 

As I dug through the source code in the F-PC system every evening for the last four months, I was constantly reminded of this old Chinese proverb: 'Foolish Old Man moving a mountain.'  Shifting through two million bytes of Forth source listings and trying to bring some sense to the whole thing is often very frustrating.  My wife kept nagging me: "When are you going to finish it and be a father to the kids?"

 

The Foolish Old Man had one definite advantage over me.  The mountain didn't move, while Tom Zimmer just kept on enhancing and sometimes made major modifications on the F-PC.  Every time I met him in the office, he would say: "Want to see something new?"  I definitely did not want to see anything new.  I just wanted to finish the documentation, but that didn't help.  Anytime I finished documenting a file, it got changed the next morning.  I shot at a moving target, and always hit its shadow.

 

We figured that the only way to get F-PC stabilized was to shoot Tom.  But he protested: "Then who's going to fix bugs in F-PC?"  Well, there went our argument.

 

The game actually started last year, 1987.  Tom distributed his systems at FIG chapters meetings.  The name of his system kept changing.  He ought to have exhausted the alphabets, going through HF, DF, PF, ZF, etc.  Bob Smith also gave me his LaForth system.  At that time I was working on my 'Forth Notebook,' Volume 2.  I promised that after I finished my book, I would start working on one of their systems.  As 1987 draw to a close, I kept on getting new disks and updates, so I figured that I might as well wait a bit longer till they settled down.  After the new year, I got trapped in coding Bach's 'The Art of Fugue.' It was a very fascinating project and I was fully occupied and incapacitated for several months.

 

Just after the new year, my working conditions at Lockheed deteriorated to such a low point that I quit.  My presence in Lockheed had never been truly appreciated.  I was only marginally tolerated because I got jobs done.  The reason I got jobs done was I insisted on doing things my way, i.e. the Forth way.  People would want them done in Fortran, in C, in Pascal, and lately in Ada.  My department manager and my supervisor tried to enlighten me on other avenues to achieve the same goal, but did not persist that I change my addiction to Forth.  The situation became unbearable after both of them were relieved of their managerial positions early in 1987.  I lost my protective shields and had to deal with reality in the company.  That ability was not engraved in my genes.

 

I found my refuge at Maxtor Corporation.  It has a very strong group of Forth programmers in its Test Engineering Department.  My primary motive in going there was that I would not have to constantly justify my using Forth as programming tool versus other languages and tools.  The real thrill is to work in the company of Tom Zimmer, Bob Smith, Jerry Modrow, Charles Curley, and many other Forth programmers in a very harmonious and courteous atmosphere.

 

After Bob Smith taught a Forth class in the company, he decided to organize a weekly technical seminar with a strong bias towards topics in Forth.  I got a chance to play ' The Art of Fugue' in one of the seminars.  In another session, Tom talked about XF, his then latest version of Forth.  In the discussion that followed, he mentioned that Maxtor did not hold any rights on the package and he was making it public domain.  I got this brilliant idea that we would release it with full documentation and call it F88.  The name was very catchy because of its F83 heritage, its segmented architecture optimized for the 80x88 CPU's, and the current year of 1988.  It would also force us to work hard so that it would be released before 1989 hits us.

 

After reaching some consensus among the Forth Mafias in Maxtor, the project was carried to the Silicon Valley FIG Chapter for support and beta testing.  An F88 Working Group was organized in a Chapter meeting and the Chapter also allocated several FORML sessions to the Working Group to arrange presentations and discussions on F88.  The response from chapter members was very favorable and many people took the diskette set for testing and evaluation.

 

However, the name F88 irritated some members because its similarity to F83 and its connotation of a Forth-88 standard.  The worst news was that Martin Tracy had it as a trade mark.  At one time, we toyed with the name FAT-Forth, which is quite appropriate because the system is fat, and it is best used on an AT class machine.  Eventually, Tom and Bob decided to use the name F-PC, which was accepted without any objection.

 

I volunteered to coordinate the documentation efforts, fully aware that coordination might mean actual writing the manual myself.  The original plan was to generate two documents to accompany the released version of F-PC: a User's Manual which explains how to use the system, and a Systems Manual to explain how the system works.  The documentation effort looked quite easy at that time because Tom had already generated a substantial amount of documentation, included in the system as on-line help and many related documentation files.  What had to be done, it seemed, was to take the existing documentation, rearrange it a little bit, add some glue and the job would be done.

 

I was particularly interested in documenting the kernel in F-PC because that was one thing I had tried to avoid in all my previous books on Forth systems, including 'Systems Guide to figFORTH' and 'Inside F83.'  The reason was that a Forth kernel must be written in assembly and it is messy.  At the same time, you cannot write about the kernel without first choosing a specific CPU.  In figFORTH, you have the choice among 8080, 6502, PACE, PDP-11, 9900, and 1802.  In F83, you have 8080, 8088/86, and 68000.  If you made a choice, it would certainly be the wrong one.  In F-PC, there is no problem because it is for 80x88 and for 80x88 only.  Since IBM with some substantial help from many Taiwanese sweat shops cornered 50% of the market place with the 80x88/86 series of personal computers, a manual focused on an 80x88 kernel should find a good size audience.

 

Additional encouragement came from Dr. S. Y. Tang, who suggested that a good guide on assembly programming in Forth could be a nice product he could use.  F-PC is a perfect vehicle to teach 80x88 assembly programming, because the assembler in Forth is interactive and there are numerous examples of code words in the F-PC kernel.  One can pull out a code word in the F-PC kernel and modify it a little to suit his problem.  Load it and get it done.  I even thought of a very catchy title: "How to succeed in Assembly Programming without Trying."  With the large number of code words in F-PC, one should be able to find enough existing code to solve any problem.

 

The F-PC User's Manual was completed in July.  The Systems Manual, however, overwhelmed me.  We missed the perfect release date, August 8, 1988, which was supposed to be the luckiest day in this century.  It was not too much of a loss, however, after losing F88 for its name.  We pushed back the release date to November, and hoped that we would distribute it at the Annual Forth Convention at Anaheim in Southern California.  The name was also changed to F-PC Technical Reference Manual.  A thick manual needs a long name.

 

I also had to reduce the scope of this Manual to meet the deadline.  The 8088 assembler, the multitasker, and the meta compiler will be documented in another volume later.  These omissions are not detrimental because they are very close to similar utilities in F83, which were fairly elucidated in "Inside F83."  Tom Zimmer's editor was still evolving and nobody wanted to do anything on it before it solidified.  Bob Smith claimed that he was exhausted implementing the floating point packages and had no energy left to write a detailed documentation for it.  We are trying to convince Mark Smiley that he should write the reference manual for the floating packages with his contributions on graphics.  We need help.

 

With the exceptions of 8088 assembler, the massive editor, the multitasker, and the meta compiler, this F-PC Technical Reference Manual contains narration on all the words inside the F-PC kernel.  The source code files include KERNEL1.SEQ to KERNEL4.SEQ, with many other files needed for the kernel to function, like console and printer input/output, file interface, and interface to the DOS operating system.  The narration follows the loading order of the source files.  Related words are grouped together in chapters and sections, which usually begin with some overviews and introductory remarks.

 

Each word is most often documented in an imaginary screen with three windows:  The top window runs between the left and right margins, containing the name and a functional description of this word.  The lower left window contains the source code presented in the vertical style.  The lower right window contains comments on the corresponding code in the left window.  The underlying desire was to make this manual loadable, if the text in the top window were commented out.  Then we would have a system in which truly complete documentation is provided with the source code.  The idea is still premature.  Nevertheless, it is not impossible if everybody were to get an optical disk drive from Maxtor...

 

To appreciate F-PC, one has to understand the reasons why Tom developed this system.  The first is to break the 64 Kbyte barrier so that large and sophisticated applicationss can be written on top of it.  The second is to provide a very smooth interface among the user, the Forth system, and the DOS operating system.  A tremendous amount of his time was spent on the editor, which becomes a very friendly word processor.  Rough corners in the assembler, debugger and many other F83 utilities were also patiently polished.  The third, but not the least, is speed.  Tom's obsession to speed lead to many strange features in F-PC, such as 64 hashed threads in vocabulary, upper case conversion through a large lookup table, flattening out a load-byte-store-word loop in the video output routine, etc.  It makes F-PC one of the fastest Forth systems for PC/XT/AT computers.

 

This Manual serves three different types of Forth users.  The advanced users would most likely concentrate on Chapter 1 and 4, which describe the virtual Forth engine, the interpreter, and the compiler.  Users who have had experience in using Forth might want to dip into 8088 assembly code to optimized an application.  Chapters 2, 3, and 6 contains numerous low level code words which are useful as templates and examples to be borrowed to construct words in assembly.  Chapters 7 and 8 are mostly utilities in colon words.  Those who are new to Forth are encouraged to scan over them for guidance in Forth programming techniques and good programming style.

 

F-PC is much more than can be covered in a single volume.  A huge number of user contributed files are included, covering a wide variety of topics.  Documentation is provided in all these files, though the degree of completeness varied from file to file.  With the knowledge on the F-PC kernel presented in this Manual, struggling through these files should be much less painful.  We certainly hope that many users can manage to build useful, even profitable, applications with the tools supplied in this system.

 

As the newest member in the tradition of public domain Forth systems, following the steps of figForth, MVP-Forth, and F83, F-PC is an effort to address many needs of the current Forth users.  Whether it addresses these needs successfully or not, only time can tell.  I hope that this Manual will help the user climb the learning curve a little bit faster and that his project can be completed a little bit earlier.

 

Let's applaud Tom Zimmer for making F-PC publicly available and also his continuing efforts in polishing it and enhancing it, though against the wishes of most users and myself in particular.  Bob Smith also deserves acknowledgement in contributing many code and ideas in its evolution.  They have also provided inspiration and encouragement to me in writing this Manual.  It is impossible to acknowledge all the people who helped in documenting the F-PC system.  However, my special thanks are due to John McKnight who carefully read through the manuscript and made numerous suggestions and comments to make this Manual easier to read and comprehend. 

 

What about that Foolish Old Man?

 

The story as told by Le-Tze is as follows:

 

The Foolish Old Man decided to move the mountain in front of his house so that he could get to places easier.  The Wise Man On The Riverbank laughed at him, saying: "The mountain is so big, and you are so old.  How much can you dig up in your lifetime?"  The Foolish Old Man answered: "Though I am old and my strength is fading, I will dig as much as I can.  After I die, my sons will continue digging.  After they die, their sons will continue digging.  And so on.  There will be many and many of us, and this mountain does not grow.  We will get rid of it eventually."

 

The Mountain God heard the conversation and was scared.  He reported to the Emperor In Heaven.  The Emperor sent two Muscle Gods and they moved the mountain out of the Foolish Old Man's way.

 

With this big mountain moved, what's in your way of using F-PC?

 

Happy Forthing!

 

 

C. H. Ting

 

San Mateo, California

October, 1988
